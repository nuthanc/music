Lesson 6 concludes the foundational section on chords by introducing a rigorous practice methodology, the essential role of rhythmic timekeepers, and the "secret" to professional-sounding strumming.

### **The "Pairs" and "5-Minute" Practice Methodology**
The instructor highlights that while playing a single chord is easy, switching between them in time with a beat is the "sad truth" or the actual difficulty of the guitar. To master this, he provides a specific regimen:
*   **Practice in Pairs:** Choose two chords (like C and G) and switch back and forth between them exclusively.
*   **The 5-Minute Rule:** You must practice these switches non-stop for five minutes to build the physical strength and endurance required for actual performances and gigs.
*   **Think Ahead:** Never focus on the chord you are currently playing; always force your mind to visualize the next shape coming up.
*   **Self-Recording:** Use a recording device to listen to yourself objectively, as players often believe they are playing better than they actually are.

### **The Role of a Timekeeper**
According to the sources, playing without a rhythmic timekeeper is "pointless" because music without rhythm is "fooling yourself".
*   **Benefits:** Using a drum machine or keyboard rhythm allows you to learn in **half the time** compared to practicing without one.
*   **Choosing a Machine:** A "satisfying" timekeeper should have good sound quality (timbre) and at least **100 different beat patterns** (like rock, waltz, or shuffle) to match various songs.
*   **The Systematic Challenge:** You should challenge yourself by increasing the strumming rate while keeping the machine at a steady speed:
    *   **Whole Notes:** Strum once every four beats.
    *   **Half Notes:** Strum every two beats.
    *   **Quarter Notes:** Strum on every beat.
    *   **Half Beats (Eighth Notes):** Strum on the beat and the "and" (1 & 2 & 3 & 4).
    *   **Quarter Beats (Sixteenth Notes):** Strum four times per beat using the syllables "1 e & a".

### **The Secret to Strumming: The "Clock" Hand**
A major takeaway is the explanation of how professional strumming patterns are executed.
*   **Constant Motion:** Your hand should act like a **pendulum or a clock**, moving in a perfectly regular down-up-down-up motion with the beat.
*   **Selective Contact ("Whiffing"):** You do not change the speed of your hand to create different rhythms. Instead, your hand moves constantly, but you only make contact with the strings on certain strokes while **"whiffing"** past them on others.
*   **Notation:** The instructor uses **"D"** for a contact downstroke, **"U"** for a contact upstroke, and a **black dot** to represent a "missed" stroke where the hand moves but the pick does not hit the strings.

### **General Practice Advice**
*   **Left-Handed Players:** The instructor suggests that left-handed individuals consider learning **right-handed**. This avoids the difficulty of finding left-handed instruments and allows players to use standard instructional charts without having to mentally flip them.
*   **Balance "Grunt Work" and Fun:** Every practice session should begin with 5–20 minutes of "grunt work" (scales and chord switches with a timekeeper) before moving on to playing songs for fun.
*   **Sequential Learning:** He reiterates that skipping lessons—such as jumping to the blues in Lesson 24 without knowing the intervals and modes from Lessons 12 and 17—will prevent you from ever seeing the "big picture" of music.
